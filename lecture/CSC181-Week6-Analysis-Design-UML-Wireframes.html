04

Modeling

Why draw models at all?

1

Communicate

A diagram shows the team and stakeholders the same picture

2

Explore

Cheaply try alternatives before writing code

3

Document

Help future maintainers understand the system

"All models are wrong, but some are useful." A model leaves things out on purpose. Draw only what helps you decide or explain.

Sketch

Quick, on a whiteboard or paper, to think and discuss. Most of your diagrams.

Blueprint

Detailed enough that someone else could build from it.

Executable

Models that generate code. Rare outside specialist domains.

G. Box (1976) · M. Fowler, UML Distilled, 3rd ed. (2003)

05

Modeling

UML: one language, many diagrams

Structural: what the system is made of

  • Class diagram: types, attributes, relationships
  • Component diagram: deployable parts and interfaces
  • Deployment diagram: where software runs
  • Object and package diagrams

Behavioural: what the system does

  • Use case diagram: actors and goals (Week 4)
  • Activity diagram: steps and decisions in a flow
  • Sequence diagram: messages between parts over time
  • State machine: how one thing changes state

For your milestone: a class or domain model, plus at least one activity, sequence, or state diagram for your most important flow.

OMG Unified Modeling Language (UML) 2.5.1

Find the domain model in your stories

Noun and verb analysis

Nouns in your stories suggest classes and attributes. Verbs suggest operations and relationships.

UI/UX design

UX and UI are not the same

User experience (UX)

The whole experience of using a product: whether it is useful, easy, and pleasant, before, during, and after use.

Can Marites find the parade route in the crowd, on prepaid data?

User interface (UI)

The screens and controls people interact with: layout, type, colour, buttons.

Is the "Near me now" button big, high-contrast, and reachable with a thumb?

Good UI cannot rescue a bad UX decision. A beautiful screen for a feature nobody needs is still a failure.

ISO 9241-210:2019, Human-centred design for interactive systems

15

UI/UX design

Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics

1

Visibility of status

Show what is happening: "Saving…", "Updated 5 min ago"

2

Match the real world

Use visitors' words, not database terms

3

User control

Easy undo and back

4

Consistency

Same thing, same look, same place

5

Error prevention

Disable "Save" until the time is valid

6

Recognition over recall

Show options; do not make people remember

7

Flexibility

Shortcuts for frequent users

8

Minimalist design

Every element earns its place

9

Help with errors

Say what went wrong and how to fix it

10

Help and docs

Easy to find, short, task-focused

J. Nielsen, "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design," Nielsen Norman Group (1994, updated 2020)

16

UI/UX design

Design for the real context of use

Thumb reach

Main actions low on the screen, where one thumb can reach

Big touch targets

At least about 44–48 px; space between them

Light pages

Prepaid data: small images, no autoplay video

Readable outdoors

High contrast, large text; never grey on grey

Plain language

Short labels; consider Filipino or Cebuano where users need it

Forgiving forms

Few fields, sensible defaults, keep input on errors

Your Week 5 non-functional requirements belong here: each guideline should trace to an NFR or a persona detail.

Wireframes

Choose the right level of fidelity

1

Sketch

Paper or whiteboard, minutes. Explore many ideas.

2

Wireframe

Grey boxes, real labels. Layout and flow.

3

Mockup

Colour, type, images. Visual design.

4

Prototype

Clickable. Test the flow with users.

Start low

People criticize a sketch freely; they hesitate to criticize something that looks finished.

Tools

Paper, Excalidraw, draw.io, Figma (free for education). Pick one the team can share.

For your milestone

Wireframes of every MVP screen, plus a clickable prototype of one key flow if you can.

Wireframes

Design every state, not just the happy one

1

Ideal

The screen with real, typical content

2

Empty

"No events in the next hour near you. Here are the next three."

3

Loading

Show cached data or a skeleton, not a blank screen

4

Partial

One event has no venue yet: what does its card show?

5

Error

Offline, server down, location denied: say what to do next

Your Week 4 unhappy-path acceptance criteria tell you which states each screen needs.

Annotate each wireframe with: the story ID, the states shown, and any accessibility notes (labels, contrast, focus order).

S. Hurff, "How to fix a bad user interface" (2015)

21

Wireframes

Test wireframes with real users

1

Pick tasks from stories

"Find an event starting soon near the plaza." Do not explain the screens first.

2

Recruit 3 to 5 people

From your stakeholder list, not your teammates.

3

Ask them to think aloud

They say what they look for and expect while they try.

4

Watch, do not help

Note where they hesitate, misread, or give up.

5

Fix and repeat

Change the wireframe; test the next person on the new version.

Record what you changed and why. It is evidence for the "user or peer feedback" part of your final defense.
