03

Why start here

Building the right thing comes before building it right

Building it right

Verification: does the software match its specification?

Code quality, tests, reviews. We cover these from Week 7 onward.

Building the right thing

Validation: does the software meet the real need?

Stakeholders, problem, scope. If this is wrong, perfect code still fails.

Errors in understanding the problem are the most expensive to fix. They are found late, usually when users finally see the product, and every phase built on them must be redone.

Remember the Week 2 ATM: the ₱200-bill request surfaced during testing. One early conversation with the bank would have caught it.

B. W. Boehm, Software Engineering Economics (1981) · Sommerville, Software Engineering, 10th ed., Ch. 4

Stakeholders

Who is a stakeholder?

Definition

Any person, group, or organization that affects, is affected by, or has an interest in the system or the project.

Users

People who operate the software directly

Customers & sponsors

People who pay for it, approve it, or own the goal

Operators & maintainers

People who run, support, and change it after launch

Regulators

Bodies whose rules it must follow

Affected non-users

People whose lives change because it exists

The development team

You: your time, skills, and grades are on the line

Stakeholders

The stakeholders teams forget

Negative stakeholders

People who lose something if the system succeeds: the printer who made the paper programs

Users with disabilities

Low vision, hearing loss, limited mobility. Accessibility is a requirement, not a polish step

Low-connectivity users

Prepaid data, weak signal in crowded venues, older phones

Data subjects

Anyone whose personal data you collect, even if they never log in

Future maintainers

Whoever runs and fixes it after your team graduates

Older and first-time users

Lolo and Lola looking for the parade route

Ask every time: who is not in the room, and what happens to them if we get this wrong?

Stakeholders

How to find your stakeholders

1

Walk the process

Trace the current way the task gets done, step by step. Who touches it at each step?

2

Follow the data

Where does information come from, who changes it, who reads it?

3

Follow the money

Who pays, who earns, who saves or loses money because of this?

4

Walk the onion

Go ring by ring, from users out to regulators and the public

5

Ask "who else?"

End every conversation with: who else should we talk to? (snowballing)

6

Check the rules

Laws, ordinances, school or office policies: who enforces them?

List roles first ("barangay secretary"), then name the real people you can reach.

Stakeholder analysis

The power–interest grid

Power: low → high

Keep satisfied

High power, low interest. Consult on key decisions; do not overload.

e.g. police and disaster office

Manage closely

High power, high interest. Involve them early and often.

e.g. festival organizers

Monitor

Low power, low interest. Check in occasionally.

e.g. the press

Keep informed

Low power, high interest. Share progress; ask for feedback.

e.g. visitors, vendors

Interest: low → high

A. Mendelow (1991) · C. Eden & F. Ackermann, Making Strategy (1998)

11

Stakeholder analysis

Stakeholders want different things

ConflictSide A wantsSide B wantsA possible resolution
VisibilityVendors: top placement in listingsVisitors: nearest or best-rated firstSort by distance; label paid placement clearly
DataOrganizers: attendance and location dataVisitors: privacy, no trackingAnonymous counts only, with opt-in consent
ChangeOrganizers: edit the schedule any timeVisitors: a schedule they can trustShow "updated 5 min ago" and notify changes
EffortOrganizers: rich features by festival dayYour team: something finished in 12 weeksAgree on an MVP; put the rest on a "later" list

Needs vs. wants

A want is a stated request ("an AR map"). A need is the underlying goal ("find the stage quickly"). Design for the need.

Make conflicts visible

Write them down and decide on purpose. Hidden conflicts come back as late change requests.

12

Stakeholder analysis

Talk to real people, early

Interviews

15 minutes, open questions: "Walk me through the last time you…"

Observation

Watch the task happen where it happens: the canteen line, the barangay hall

Short surveys

Good for "how many" questions once you know what to ask

Existing evidence

Complaints, Facebook comments, logbooks, forms in use today

Ask about the past, not the future

"What did you do last time?" beats "Would you use an app that…?" People are poor at predicting their own behaviour.

Respect the people you ask

Explain why you are asking, get consent, collect no personal data you do not need, and never invent responses.

Classmates can stand in for users during practice, but your proposal should cite at least a few conversations with real stakeholders.

13

Stakeholder analysis

Personas make users concrete

Persona

Marites, 34, visiting from Cagayan de Oro

Context: day trip with two kids; Android phone on prepaid data; signal drops near the plaza.

Goals: catch the street-dance showdown; find food and a restroom fast.

Frustrations: schedules spread across many Facebook posts; times change without notice.

"I just want to know what is happening near me in the next hour."

What a persona is

A short, specific portrait of a typical user, built from research, used to keep decisions focused on real needs.

Use it to decide

Would Marites need this feature? Would it work on her phone, in that crowd?

Do not invent it

A persona with no research behind it is a guess wearing a name tag.

A. Cooper, The Inmates Are Running the Asylum (1999)

14

Problem framing

Start with the problem, not the solution

Solution-first

"We'll build a Diyandi app with an AR map, chat, and ticketing."

Starts from a feature list. Nobody has checked who needs it, or why.

Problem-first

"Out-of-town visitors miss events because schedules are scattered and change without notice."

Starts from a person and a pain. Many solutions are possible, and some are not apps.

"People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole." Users care about the outcome, not your technology.

If the only possible answer to your problem statement is your app, you have written a solution, not a problem.

15

Problem framing

Dig for the root cause: the 5 Whys

Symptom

Visitors miss events.

Why?

They do not know when or where events are.

Why?

Schedules are posted across many Facebook pages and posters.

Why?

Each organizing group publishes its own updates separately.

Why?

There is no single, updatable source of the official schedule.

Root cause found

A single source of truth the organizers can update. That is a problem software can address.

Stop when it is actionable

Five is a guide, not a rule. Stop when the answer is something you can change.

T. Ohno, Toyota Production System (1988)

Problem framing

A problem statement template

Template

The problem of [what] affects [whom], the impact of which is [what happens]. A successful solution would [measurable benefit].

PartDiyandi festival guide
The problem offestival schedules being scattered across many pages and changing without notice
affectsout-of-town and local visitors, and the organizers who field their questions
the impact of which ismissed events, crowding at the wrong venue, and repeated questions to organizers
a successful solution wouldlet a visitor find what is on near them in the next hour in under 30 seconds, with times no older than 15 minutes

Problem-statement format from the Rational Unified Process (RUP) Vision document

Problem framing

Weak and strong problem statements

WeakWhat is wrongStronger
"There is no app for the festival."A missing solution, not a problem"Visitors miss events because schedules are scattered and change without notice."
"People have a hard time."Who? Doing what?"Out-of-town visitors cannot tell which events are near them right now."
"Barangay services are slow."Vague, no evidence"Residents wait 2–3 hours for a barangay clearance; we observed this on two weekday mornings."
"Students want a better canteen."A want, not a measurable need"Students lose 25 minutes of a 60-minute lunch break queuing; success means under 10."

Problem framing

Separate evidence from assumptions

Evidence

  • We observed it: watched the line, timed the wait
  • They told us: interview notes, survey answers
  • It is recorded: complaints, logbooks, posts
  • It is published: reports, news, statistics with a source

Assumption

  • "Everyone has a smartphone."
  • "Organizers will keep the data updated."
  • "Visitors will install another app."
  • "The barangay will let us use their records."

Keep an assumption log. Write each assumption down, mark how risky it is, and plan how you will check it. Untested assumptions become Week 5 risks.

GoalSuccess measureHow we will check

Specific and measurable

A number, a time, a count, or a yes/no check.

Realistic for 12 weeks

Measurable by your team, with access you actually have.

Tied to the problem

Each goal answers part of the problem statement.

Scope

Scope is a fence around the project

Definition

Scope is the boundary between what the project will deliver and what it will not.

Product scope

The features and functions of the software.

"Visitors can browse today's events by venue and time."

Project scope

The work needed to deliver it: documents, testing, training, deployment.

"We will write a user guide and test with 5 users."

An "out of scope" list is as important as the "in scope" list. It is what stops arguments in Week 9.

Product vs. project scope: PMI, PMBOK Guide

21

Scope · example

In, out, and later: the Diyandi festival guide

In scope

  • Browse events by day, venue, and time
  • See what is on near me in the next hour
  • Organizer page to add and edit events
  • Show when each event was last updated
  • Last-loaded schedule works offline

Out of scope

  • Ticket sales and payments
  • Live GPS tracking of floats
  • Chat between visitors
  • Native iOS and Android apps
  • Official PNP alerts

Later, if time allows

  • Vendor listings and map pins
  • Favourites and reminders
  • Filipino and Cebuano language options
  • Simple attendance counts with consent

Every "out" item has a reason: payments need legal and banking work, GPS needs hardware, alerts need official authority.

Scope

A context diagram draws the boundary

Inside the box is what you build. Outside are people and systems you exchange information with but do not build.

Context diagram (level-0 DFD): T. DeMarco, Structured Analysis and System Specification (1978)

23

Scope

Start with a minimum viable product

Minimum viable product (MVP)

The smallest version that delivers real value to real users and tests your riskiest assumption.

Release 1 · MVP

Today's events by venue and time; organizer edit page

Release 2

Near-me filter; last-updated label; offline cache

Release 3

Vendor listings; favourites; languages

Each release is usable

Like Kniberg's skateboard → bicycle → car, not wheel → axle → chassis.

Not a half-built product

A login screen and a database with no useful feature is not an MVP.

E. Ries, The Lean Startup (2011) · H. Kniberg, "Making sense of MVP" (2016)

24

Scope

Scope creep and gold-plating

Scope creep

Requirements keep growing after scope is agreed, without adjusting time or resources.

"While you're at it, can it also sell tickets?"

Gold-plating

The team adds features nobody asked for, because they are fun or impressive.

"Let's add dark mode and animations before the schedule works."

Write scope down

In, out, and later, agreed by the team and the instructor

Use a change rule

Something new comes in only if something of similar size goes out or moves to "later"

Check against the problem

Does this request help solve the problem statement?

Scope

Right-sizing a semester project

Good signs

  • One primary user group
  • One core workflow done well
  • Stakeholders you can actually reach
  • Data you can get or safely simulate
  • A demo you can run in 10 minutes

Warning signs

  • "Facebook / Grab / Shopee for X"
  • Real payments or real money
  • Hardware, sensors, or live GPS
  • Machine learning from scratch
  • Needs data or approval you cannot get

Small and finished beats big and broken. A well-tested MVP with clear documentation scores better than an ambitious prototype that crashes in the demo.

Feasibility

Can we build it? Five feasibility questions

T

Technical

Do we have, or can we learn, the technology and skills?

E

Economic

Do the benefits justify the cost? Can we afford the tools?

L

Legal

Does it comply with laws, licenses, and policies?

O

Operational

Will people actually use it and keep it running?

S

Schedule

Can it be done by the deadline?

✓

Verdict

Go, go with changes, or no-go

A feasibility study answers one question: is this project worth starting, as scoped?

TELOS feasibility dimensions · Sommerville, Software Engineering, 10th ed., Ch. 4

Feasibility

What to do with a “no”

Cut the feature

Live GPS of floats is not feasible: drop it, keep the static route map

Shrink the scope

Serve one festival day or one district first

Simulate the hard part

No real data yet? Use clearly labelled sample data

Run a spike

Unsure if a library works? Spend two days finding out

Change the users

Organizers too busy? Start with one cultural group

Pivot the problem

If no version is feasible, pick another problem in the same domain

Finding a no-go in Week 3 costs a few hours. Finding it in Week 9 costs the project.
