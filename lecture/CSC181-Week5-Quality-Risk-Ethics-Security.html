03

Why quality matters

It works, but nobody uses it

Functionally correct

The Diyandi guide lists every event, with the right times and venues.

All the user stories pass their acceptance criteria.

But in the real plaza

It takes 20 seconds to load on prepaid data, crashes when 5,000 people open it at once, and the grey-on-white text is unreadable in sunlight.

Visitors give up and go back to Facebook.

Quality attributes decide whether software is usable in its real context. They also shape the architecture more than any single feature does.

Recall Week 4: functional requirements say what; non-functional requirements say how well.

Quality attributes

The ISO/IEC 25010 product quality model

Functional suitability

Complete, correct, appropriate functions

Performance efficiency

Response time, resource use, capacity

Compatibility

Works with other systems and coexists

Usability

Learnable, operable, accessible, error-protecting

Reliability

Available, fault-tolerant, recoverable

Security

Confidentiality, integrity, accountability

Maintainability

Modular, analysable, modifiable, testable

Portability

Adaptable, installable, replaceable

Not every attribute matters equally. Pick the two or three that would make or break your project.

Risk

What is a risk?

Definition

An uncertain event or condition that, if it happens, affects the project’s objectives. Risk exposure = probability × impact.

Project risks

Affect the schedule or resources. A teammate drops the course.

Product risks

Affect the quality of the software. Offline caching does not work on older phones.

Business risks

Affect the organization or stakeholders. Organizers adopt a different app.

A risk might happen. An issue has already happened. Risks you ignore become issues.

Sommerville, Software Engineering, 10th ed., Ch. 22 · PMI, PMBOK Guide

Risk

The risk management process

1

Identify

Brainstorm what could go wrong

2

Analyze

Rate probability and impact

3

Plan

Choose a response and an owner

4

Monitor

Review every week; watch triggers

New risks appear and old ones fade. Revisit the register at every sprint review.

Identify early

Week 5 is the cheapest time: nothing is built yet.

Assign an owner

Every risk has one person who watches it.

Keep it short

8 to 12 well-written risks beat 40 vague ones.

B. W. Boehm, "Software Risk Management: Principles and Practices," IEEE Software (1991)

Risk

Four ways to respond to a risk

1

Avoid

Change the plan so the risk cannot happen. Drop live GPS, so GPS hardware cannot fail.

2

Mitigate

Reduce probability or impact. Load-test the site in Week 9; cache pages on a CDN.

3

Transfer

Shift it to someone better able to handle it. Use managed hosting instead of running our own server.

4

Accept

Live with it, with a contingency plan. If the map service changes terms, fall back to venue addresses.

A contingency plan is what you will do if the risk happens. A trigger tells you when to start it.

Risk

Writing a good risk statement

WeakWhyStronger
"Time."Not an event; no consequence"If exams overlap Sprint 2, then testing is cut and defects reach the demo."
"The server might crash."No cause, no effect on goals"If the free tier sleeps after inactivity, then the first visitor waits 30 s."
"Bad teamwork."Vague; cannot be monitored"If PRs wait more than 2 days for review, then merges pile up and conflicts grow."
"Security."A category, not a risk"If an organizer password is guessed, then a fake schedule is published."

Format: If <cause or condition>, then <consequence for the project or users>.

Privacy

Privacy: the Data Privacy Act of 2012

Republic Act 10173

Applies to anyone who processes personal information in the Philippines, including student projects that collect data from real people.

1

Transparency

Tell people what you collect, why, and how it is used

2

Legitimate purpose

Collect only for a declared, lawful purpose

3

Proportionality

Collect only what is necessary for that purpose

Sensitive personal information needs extra care: health, government ID numbers, religion, age, and more. Avoid collecting it unless the project truly needs it.

Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173) and its Implementing Rules · National Privacy Commission

18

Privacy

Privacy by design for student projects

1

Minimize

Ask: do we need this field at all? A festival guide needs no birthdate.

2

Ask for consent

Plain-language notice and an opt-in before collecting personal data or location.

3

Limit access

Only the people who need data can see it; organizers cannot see visitor locations.

4

Use fake data for testing

Never put classmates' or residents' real data in test databases or screenshots.

5

Delete when done

Decide how long data is kept, and remove it after the course.

6

Keep it off GitHub

No real personal data, exports, or database dumps in the repository.

A. Cavoukian, "Privacy by Design: The 7 Foundational Principles" (2009)

Security

Security basics: the CIA triad

C

Confidentiality

Only authorized people can see the data. Organizer contact details are not public.

I

Integrity

Data is not changed without authorization. Only the owner can edit an event.

A

Availability

The system works when people need it. The guide stays up on festival night.

Authentication

Proving who you are: login, passwords, one-time codes.

Authorization

What you are allowed to do once logged in: roles and permissions.

Accountability

Recording who did what: "edited by Ana at 7:42 PM".

Security

Threat modeling in four questions

QuestionDiyandi guide
1. What are we building?Public schedule pages, an organizer login, an edit page, a database, a map link
2. What can go wrong?Organizer account taken over → fake schedule. Edit page reachable without login. Database key leaked in the repository.
3. What will we do about it?Strong passwords and rate limits; server-side permission checks; secrets in environment variables, never committed
4. Did we do a good job?Try to edit an event while logged out; run a secret scan; review in Week 10

Spend 30 minutes as a team on these four questions. Add what you find to your risk register.

A. Shostack, Threat Modeling: Designing for Security (2014)

22

Security

Security habits for your repository

Never commit secrets

API keys and passwords go in a .env file listed in .gitignore

If you leak one, rotate it

Deleting the commit is not enough; the key is in the history. Revoke it and make a new one.

Turn on GitHub protections

Secret scanning, Dependabot alerts, and branch protection on main

Use the framework's auth

Do not write your own password hashing or session handling

Validate on the server

Client-side checks are for convenience; the server must re-check

Least privilege

Each account and key gets only the access it needs

24

Accessibility

WCAG: four principles (POUR)

P

Perceivable

Text alternatives for images; captions; enough colour contrast; no information by colour alone

O

Operable

Everything works with a keyboard; no time limits that cannot be extended; visible focus

U

Understandable

Plain language; predictable navigation; clear error messages with fixes

R

Robust

Valid HTML and proper labels so screen readers and other tools can interpret it

Target WCAG 2.2 Level AA. It is the level most laws and organizations require.

W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 (2023)

25

Accessibility

A practical accessibility checklist

Check while you build

  • Text contrast at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text)
  • Every image has meaningful alt text, or empty alt if decorative
  • Every form field has a visible label
  • Status is never shown by colour alone
  • Pages still work at 200% zoom

Test before each sprint review

  • Use the whole flow with the keyboard only
  • Run axe, WAVE, or Lighthouse accessibility checks
  • Try a screen reader: TalkBack (Android) or NVDA (Windows)
  • Read error messages aloud: do they say how to fix it?
  • Ask someone outside the team to try it

Automated tools catch only some problems. A keyboard and screen-reader check finds what they miss.

Ethics

Responsible AI use in your project

Disclose

State which AI tools you used, for what, and how you checked the output

Verify

Test and understand every line of AI-suggested code before you commit it

Protect data

Never paste personal data, interview notes with names, or secrets into AI tools

If your app uses AI

Tell users, handle wrong answers, and avoid decisions that harm people without human review

Watch for bias

Check whether outputs treat groups of users differently

Respect licenses

Generated code can resemble licensed code; review it like any other source

You are responsible for everything you submit, whoever or whatever helped write it.
