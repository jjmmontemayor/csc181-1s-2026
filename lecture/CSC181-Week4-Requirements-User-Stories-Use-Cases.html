Requirements engineering

What is a requirement?

Definition

A statement of a capability or condition a system must have to solve a stakeholder’s problem or meet an agreement, written so it can be verified.

User requirements

Plain-language statements of what users need to do. "Visitors can see what is on near them."

System requirements

Detailed, precise statements of what the system does. "The system shall list events within 1 km starting in the next 60 minutes."

Constraints

Limits on how it is built. "Must run in a mobile browser; no paid services."

ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 · Sommerville, Software Engineering, 10th ed., Ch. 4

Requirements engineering

Functional, non-functional, and constraints

Functional

  • What the system does
  • Inputs, outputs, behaviour
  • Visitors can filter events by venue.
  • Organizers can change an event time.

Non-functional

  • How well it does it
  • Performance, usability, security, reliability
  • Search results appear within 2 seconds on 3G.
  • Readable at 200% zoom.

Constraints

  • Limits on the solution
  • Technology, law, budget, standards
  • Must comply with the Data Privacy Act.
  • Free hosting only.

This week focuses on functional requirements. Week 5 goes deep on quality attributes and non-functional requirements.

Eliciting requirements

Asking good interview questions

Ask

  • "Walk me through the last time you…"
  • "What was the hardest part?"
  • "What do you do when that happens?"
  • "What have you tried already?"
  • "Who else should we talk to?"

Avoid

  • "Would you use an app that…?" (hypothetical)
  • "Don't you think a map would help?" (leading)
  • "Do you want notifications?" (yes/no)
  • "What features do you want?" (asks them to design)
  • Pitching your idea before listening

Listen for problems, workarounds, and numbers. "I check three Facebook pages and still miss things" is worth more than "an app would be nice."

Writing requirements

What makes a requirement good

1

Necessary

Traces to a real stakeholder need

2

Unambiguous

Only one way to read it

3

Verifiable

You can test whether it is met

4

Feasible

Can be built with your time and tools

5

Singular

States one thing, not three joined by "and"

6

Consistent

Does not contradict another requirement

7

Complete

Nothing a builder must guess

8

Traceable

Has an ID and a source

The two that fail most often: unambiguous and verifiable. If you cannot write a test for it, rewrite it.

Characteristics of well-formed requirements: ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018

11

Writing requirements

Hunt for weak words

WeakProblemBetter
"The app shall be fast."Fast for whom, doing what?"Search results appear within 2 s on a 3G connection."
"The app shall be user-friendly."Not testable"A first-time visitor finds tonight's main event in under 30 s."
"Show relevant events."Relevant by what rule?"Show events within 1 km that start in the next 60 minutes."
"Organizers can easily update events etc."Vague and open-ended"Organizers can change an event's time, venue, or description."
"Support all devices."Impossible to verify"Works on Chrome for Android 10+ and current desktop browsers."

Weak words to flag: fast, easy, user-friendly, flexible, robust, relevant, appropriate, etc., and/or, all, some, should be able to.

Writing requirements

EARS: five sentence patterns for clear requirements

PatternTemplateExample
UbiquitousThe <system> shall <response>.The guide shall display event times in Philippine Time.
Event-drivenWhen <trigger>, the <system> shall…When an organizer changes an event time, the guide shall notify visitors who saved it.
State-drivenWhile <state>, the <system> shall…While the device is offline, the guide shall show the last-loaded schedule.
Unwanted behaviourIf <condition>, then the <system> shall…If an event has no venue, then the guide shall hide it from the map view.
Optional featureWhere <feature>, the <system> shall…Where location access is allowed, the guide shall sort events by distance.

A. Mavin et al., "Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax (EARS)," IEEE RE (2009)

User stories

The user story format

Template

As a <role>, I want <goal>, so that <benefit>.

Role

A real stakeholder from your Week 3 list or persona: out-of-town visitor, not "user".

Goal

What they want to do, in their words: see what is on near me in the next hour.

Benefit

Why it matters, tied to the problem: so that I don't miss events while walking around.

As an out-of-town visitor, I want to see what is on near me in the next hour, so that I don't miss events while walking around.

Connextra format · M. Cohn, User Stories Applied (2004)

14

User stories

A story is a promise of a conversation: the 3 Cs

1

Card

A short written story, small enough for an index card or a GitHub issue title

2

Conversation

The details are worked out by talking with the product owner and stakeholders

3

Confirmation

Acceptance criteria that confirm when the story is done

A user story is not a full specification. The card is a reminder; the conversation fills in the details; the confirmation makes it testable.

In your repository: the card is the issue title and story, the conversation is in the issue comments, and the confirmation is a checklist of acceptance criteria.

R. Jeffries, "Essential XP: Card, Conversation, Confirmation" (2001)

15

User stories

INVEST: checking a story

I

Independent

Can be built in any order, without waiting for another story

N

Negotiable

Details are open for discussion, not a fixed contract

V

Valuable

Delivers something a stakeholder cares about

E

Estimable

The team can roughly size it

S

Small

Fits in one sprint, ideally a few days of work

T

Testable

Has clear acceptance criteria

"Valuable" is about the user. "Set up the database" is a task, not a story; it has no value on its own to a visitor.

B. Wake, "INVEST in Good Stories, and SMART Tasks" (2003)

User stories

Splitting big stories (epics)

Epic

A story too big for one sprint. Split it until each piece is small, valuable, and testable.

Split byEpic: "As a visitor, I want to find events"
Workflow stepbrowse today's list → open an event → get directions
Rule or variationfilter by venue · filter by time · filter by category
Rolevisitor view · organizer preview of their own events
Simple, then complexlist sorted by time first; sorted by distance later
Happy path, then errorsevent found; then no events, offline, location denied

Adapted from R. Lawrence, "Patterns for Splitting User Stories" (2009)

Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria say when a story is done

Definition

Conditions a story must satisfy to be accepted by the product owner. Each one is specific and testable.

Story

As an out-of-town visitor, I want to see what is on near me in the next hour, so that I don't miss events while walking around.

Acceptance criteria

  • Shows events within 1 km that start in the next 60 minutes
  • Sorted by start time, soonest first
  • Each item shows name, venue, start time, and distance
  • If location is denied, asks the visitor to pick a venue area instead
  • If no events match, says so and shows the next three events

Scenario 1: events nearbyScenario 2: location denied
Givenit is 7:00 PM and two events within 1 km start before 8:00 PMthe visitor has blocked location access
Whenthe visitor opens "Near me now"the visitor opens "Near me now"
Thenboth events are listed, soonest first, with distance shownthe guide asks them to choose a venue area, and lists events there

Given

The starting state or context.

When

The action or event.

Then

The observable result you can check.

Given–When–Then reads like a test script. Later it can become an automated test with tools such as Cucumber.

D. North, "Introducing BDD" (2006)

21

Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria vs. Definition of Done

Acceptance criteria

Specific to one story. What this feature must do.

Shows events within 1 km in the next 60 minutes.

Definition of Done

Applies to every story. The team's quality bar.

Code reviewed in a pull request · tests pass · works on a phone · README updated.

A story is done when it meets its acceptance criteria and the team's Definition of Done.

Write your team's Definition of Done this week. You will apply it from Sprint 1.

Use cases: actors, goals, and a boundary

An actor is a role outside the system. A use case is a goal the actor achieves with the system. The box is the scope from Week 3.

The product backlog

The product backlog

Definition

An ordered list of everything the product might need, owned by the product owner. The top is detailed and ready; the bottom is rough.

D

Detailed appropriately

Top items are small with acceptance criteria; lower items can be epics

E

Estimated

Each item has a rough size, even just S / M / L

E

Emergent

Items are added, split, and removed as the team learns

P

Prioritized

Ordered by value and risk; the top goes into the next sprint

R. Pichler, Agile Product Management with Scrum (2010) · The Scrum Guide (2020)

26

The product backlog

MoSCoW prioritization

Must have

  • Today's list by time
  • Event details page
  • Organizer can edit events
  • Last-updated label

Should have

  • Filter by venue
  • Save an event
  • Offline schedule

Could have

  • Near-me sorting
  • Notify on change
  • Share an event

Won't have (this time)

  • Ticket sales
  • Live GPS of floats
  • Visitor chat

Rule of thumb: Must-haves should take no more than about 60% of your capacity, leaving room for surprises.

MoSCoW: D. Clegg (1994) · DSDM / Agile Business Consortium
