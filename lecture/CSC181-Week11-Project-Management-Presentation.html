04

Project management

Scope, time, and cost: something has to give

Traditional plan

Scope is fixed up front.

Time and cost are estimated from it, and slip when the estimate was wrong.

Late surprises mean overtime or a late delivery.

Agile plan, and this course

Time and team are fixed: 12 weeks, your members.

Scope is estimated, and flexes by priority.

Late surprises mean cutting the lowest-priority stories.

Quality is not the variable to cut. Ship fewer features that work, not more features that break.

Estimation

Why early estimates miss: the cone of uncertainty

Early is a range

At the concept stage, real effort can be a quarter to four times the guess.

It narrows by working

Decisions and finished sprints remove unknowns.

Optimism is built in

The planning fallacy: we picture the best case and call it the plan.

S. McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (2006)

08

Estimation

Estimation techniques

TechniqueHowBest for
DecompositionBreak into small tasks; estimate each; add them upAnything bigger than a day
AnalogyCompare with similar work you already finishedWhen you have history: your log
Three-pointOptimistic, most likely, pessimistic; take a weighted averageUncertain tasks; gives a range
Planning pokerEveryone sizes at once; discuss the outliersStories, as a team (Week 8)
VelocityPoints finished per sprint predict the next sprintForecasting how much fits

Give a range and a confidence, not a single number: "4 to 7 hours, probably 5".

09

Estimation · example

A three-point estimate

Task 2.2 · user guide with screenshots

Optimistic

3 h

Most likely

6 h

Pessimistic

15 h

Expected = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6 = (3 + 24 + 15) ÷ 6 = 7 h

Spread = (P − O) ÷ 6 = 2 h, so plan for about 7 to 9 h.

Why the pessimistic case

Screenshots go stale if the UI changes; someone must test the guide with a user.

Not the most likely

The expected value is above the most likely, because things go wrong more than right.

Then check it

Log the actual hours and compare next time.

Monitoring

Risks and changes in the final week

Review the risk register

  • Close risks that did not happen
  • Raise new ones: demo network, a member falling ill, hosting limits
  • Each top risk has an owner and a fallback
  • Bring the register to the defense as evidence

Control late changes

  • Feature freeze: no new stories after Week 10
  • A new request is logged as an issue, not coded
  • Ask: is it a fix, or a feature?
  • "Good idea; it is in our next steps" is a complete answer

Scope creep is many small "just one more thing" additions, each reasonable, that together sink the release.

Team health: handle problems early and fairly

SituationDo firstIf it continues
A member is quiet and has no commitsAsk privately what is blocking them; offer to pairAgree specific tasks and dates at stand-up
Two members disagree on a designWrite both options as an ADR; decide on evidenceAsk the adviser to hear both sides

Tell a story, not a feature list

Feature tour

Presentation and demo

A demo that survives Murphy’s law

1

Script it

Exact clicks and inputs, written down, rehearsed

2

Prepare the data

Seed events and test accounts that tell the story; reset before the demo

3

Own the network

Test on the venue Wi-Fi; have a phone hotspot ready

4

Have a backup

A recorded video of the demo and screenshots in the slides

5

Make it visible

Zoom in, large fonts, notifications off, one browser tab

6

Recover calmly

If it fails: say so, switch to the backup, keep going

Demo from the deployed v1.0.0, not from a laptop branch. It is also proof the release works.

21

Presentation and demo

Slides that support, not compete

Do

One idea per slide, as a headline sentence

Diagrams, the board, test results: evidence

Text large enough for the back row (28 px or more)

Slide numbers, so the panel can refer back

Avoid

Paragraphs read aloud

Screenshots of code

Tiny architecture diagrams with every class

Animations and transitions that slow you down

The panel can read or listen, not both. If a slide needs reading, pause and let them read.

M. Alley, The Craft of Scientific Presentations, 2nd ed. (2013)

Presentation and demo

Answering questions in a defense

Question typeExampleGood answer
Why"Why a web app and not a mobile app?"The decision, the reason, the trade-off. Point to the ADR.
What if"What if 10,000 people open it at once?"What happens now, what you measured, what you would change.
Show me"Where are the tests for editing?"Open the repository and show them. Evidence beats claims.
Who did"Ben, explain the API you wrote."Ben answers directly, showing the code.
Gap"Did you test with screen readers?"Honest: "Partly. TalkBack on the list only; it is in known issues."

Listen, pause, answer briefly, and stop. "I don't know, but here is how I would find out" is a strong answer.

Reflection

Peer feedback that helps

Situation

When and where it happened.

"During the Sprint 2 bug bash…"

Behaviour

What they did, observable and specific.

"…you wrote clear steps for every bug you found…"

Impact

The effect on you, the team, or the product.

"…so we fixed five of six in one afternoon."

Balanced

At least one strength and one thing to improve for each teammate.

About work, not personality

"Missed two stand-ups" not "lazy".

Fair and private

Based on evidence; shared with the instructor, not used to settle scores.
